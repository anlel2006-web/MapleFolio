How accounts are taxed

TFSA: no deduction; growth and withdrawals are tax-free. RRSP: contributions are deductible and withdrawals are taxed as income. FHSA: contributions are deductible and qualifying home-purchase withdrawals are tax-free. RESP: no deduction; grants and growth are taxed in the student’s hands when paid out. Taxable accounts: interest, dividends and gains are taxed each year, and the rate depends on the type of income and your province. US withholding (15%) depends on the account and on how you hold the investment. Details are in the Account tax guide. US accounts (401(k), IRA, Roth IRA) are US retirement plans and their rules are not covered here. General education, not tax advice.

Add up every TFSA you have, at every bank or broker: room belongs to you, not to each account. Contributions means money you put in, not what the account is worth. Room starts the year you turn 18 (earliest 2009) and only counts years you lived in Canada, so enter the year you became a resident if it was later.

  • Contributions use up room right away. Withdrawals are added back on January 1 of the next year, not right away.
  • Investment gains and losses do not change your room. Only what you put in and take out counts, so a loss never gives you room back.
  • Going over your room costs 1% of the highest excess amount in each month, from the first dollar. TFSAs have no $2,000 buffer.

Account tax guide

How Canada taxes each kind of account, with the rules taken from CRA and Department of Finance Canada pages (links under Sources at the bottom). General education, not tax advice. Rules, limits and rates change, and your province and personal situation decide what applies to you.

US dividend withholding: direct holdings and ETFs

When a US company pays a dividend to someone in Canada, the US withholds tax before the money is paid. The Canada–US tax treaty caps that tax on regular (portfolio) dividends at 15%. The treaty also exempts dividends paid to pension and retirement arrangements, which is how RRSPs and RRIFs can avoid it. So whether the 15% applies depends on two things: the account the dividend lands in and the structure you hold the investment through.

  • The treaty exemption is written for pension and retirement arrangements. TFSAs, FHSAs and RESPs are not retirement arrangements, so the 15% treaty rate is what generally applies in them. Ask your broker how it treats your specific account.
  • Funds that hold other funds can have tax taken at more than one level. A fund’s prospectus and annual report show how it is built.
  • Stocks outside the US are taxed by their own countries, often at the fund level. That tax is generally not recoverable in a registered account.
  • To get the 15% treaty rate (instead of the standard US rate of 30%), brokers normally need a W-8BEN form on file.
  • REIT distributions and some other payments can be taxed differently from ordinary dividends, so check the specific security.

Canadian dividends: eligible and non-eligible

Dividends from taxable Canadian corporations are grossed up (you report more than the cash you received) and then you claim a dividend tax credit. The size of both depends on whether the paying corporation designates the dividend as eligible or non-eligible. You do not choose: your T5 or T3 slip shows the amounts.

  • The grossed-up amount, not the cash dividend, counts in your net income, which can affect income-tested benefits and credits.
  • Foreign dividends are not grossed up and get no dividend tax credit. They are fully taxable, with a possible foreign tax credit.
  • Inside a TFSA, RRSP, FHSA or RESP the gross-up and credit do not matter, because dividends are not taxed as they are earned.

Taxable accounts: income types and provinces

  • Interest is fully taxable at your marginal rate in the year it is earned.
  • Canadian dividends follow the gross-up and credit rules above.
  • Foreign income is fully taxable. Foreign tax paid can be claimed as a foreign tax credit (federal Form T2209, line 40500; provincial Form T2036 outside Quebec). The credit is limited, so the amount withheld is not always the amount you can claim.
  • Capital gains are taxed when you sell. The CRA’s inclusion rate is one-half, so 50% of the gain is added to income and taxed at your marginal rate. Capital losses offset gains, and unused net losses can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely.
  • Return of capital in a fund distribution is not taxed when paid, but it lowers your adjusted cost base, which raises the gain when you sell.
  • Province matters. You pay federal tax plus provincial or territorial tax. Each province and territory sets its own brackets, rates, credits and dividend tax credits, so the same income can be taxed differently depending on where you lived on December 31. Quebec has its own provincial return and rules, and some provinces add surtaxes or health premiums (Ontario does).

RRSP and TFSA in brief

  • RRSP: contributions are deductible and growth is tax-deferred. Withdrawals are added to your income in the year you take them. The 2026 dollar limit is $33,810 (CRA also lists $35,390 for 2027), or 18% of last year’s earned income if lower, plus unused room.
  • TFSA: contributions are not deductible, and growth and withdrawals are tax-free. See the TFSA calculator above for how room works.

FHSA (First Home Savings Account)

  • Who can open one: a Canadian resident who is at least 18 (19 where that is the age of majority) and 71 or younger on December 31 of the year of opening, and a first-time home buyer. That means you did not live in a home you owned as your main residence in the current calendar year or the previous four, and you either have no spouse or common-law partner or did not live in a home they owned in that period.
  • Limits: $8,000 of room in the year you open it, then $8,000 a year, with unused room carried forward up to $8,000 (so up to $16,000 in one year). Lifetime limit $40,000. Room starts only after you open your first FHSA and applies to all your FHSAs combined. Going over costs 1% a month on the highest excess in the month.
  • Deductions: contributions are generally deductible in the year or any later year, up to $40,000 for life. Unused contributions carry forward. Transfers from an RRSP are not deductible. Contributions in the first 60 days of a year cannot be deducted for the prior year (unlike RRSPs), and contributions after your first qualifying withdrawal cannot be deducted at all.
  • Qualifying withdrawals are tax-free and do not have to be repaid. You must be a first-time home buyer at the time (no home owned as your main residence in the current year, except the last 30 days, or the previous four), have a written agreement to buy or build a qualifying home in Canada that closes before October 1 of the following year, not have bought it more than 30 days before, stay a Canadian resident, intend to live in it within a year, and file Form RC725 with your FHSA issuer. Other withdrawals are taxable income. A qualifying withdrawal does not remove an excess amount.
  • Closing: the account must close by the earliest of 15 years after opening, the year you turn 71, or the year after your first qualifying withdrawal. Direct transfers to an RRSP or RRIF have no immediate tax; a transfer to a TFSA or RESP counts as a taxable withdrawal.

RESP (Registered Education Savings Plan)

  • Contribution rules: no annual limit, but a $50,000 lifetime limit per beneficiary across all RESPs. Contributions are not deductible. Going over the limit costs 1% a month on the excess. The beneficiary needs a Social Insurance Number to receive grants.
  • Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG): 20% on the first $2,500 contributed each year for a child, up to $500 a year. Unused grant room carries forward, so up to $1,000 of grant (on $5,000 contributed) can be paid in one year. Lifetime maximum $7,200 per child. Grants are available until the end of the year the child turns 17, with extra contribution conditions at 16 and 17. Lower- and middle-income families can get an additional 10% or 20% on the first $500 a year, which does not carry forward. If the money is not used for education, grants generally have to be returned.
  • How withdrawals are taxed:
    • Contributions come back tax-free (they were made with after-tax money).
    • Educational assistance payments (EAPs) are the grants and growth. They are taxed in the student’s hands, and many students owe little or no tax.
    • If no beneficiary goes on to post-secondary education, growth paid to the subscriber (an accumulated income payment) is taxed at the subscriber’s regular rate plus an additional 20% tax (12% in Quebec). Some of it can be moved to an RRSP if there is room, which avoids the extra 20%.
